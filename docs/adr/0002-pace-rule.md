# Pace is one median per learner, and slow is 1.5 times it

The 3-second limit went when typed answers replaced answers said aloud, so a fast outcome and a slow one are split by the learner's own pace. Pace is the median of the learner's last 60 right answer times, across all tables, recalculated after each right answer. A right answer is slow when its answer time is over 1.5 × pace and is at least 3 seconds; otherwise it is fast. A wrong answer is missed whatever its time. Until there are 20 right answers there is no pace and every right answer is fast. An answer time is capped at 20 seconds, a presentation during which the app went to the background counts as 20 seconds, and an answer time at the cap is graded like any other but does not feed pace. An answer is graded against the pace as it stood before that answer. The levels, the level changes and the draw weights are unchanged, and levels earned under the 3-second self-graded rule are carried as they are.

## Considered options

- **One pace per table, per fact, or per answer length.** Per table and per fact compare a hesitant fact only with itself: a new table sets a slow pace and then grades its own slow answers fast, so levels reach 4 while the learner still hesitates. One pace across all tables makes slow mean "slower than this learner usually is", which keeps new facts at a low level while known ones set the line. The cost is typing: a three-digit product takes more key presses, and in a simulated slow typist those facts drew more slow outcomes and sat at level 2 or 3 for longer without sticking at the bottom. Pace per answer length (1, 2 or 3 digits) is the fallback if that shows up in practice; it was set aside because the three-digit window covers 7 facts and fills slowly.
- **All answers, or only the first presentation of a fact in a drill.** Wrong answers say nothing about recall speed and quick guesses would lower pace. Repeats within a drill stay in because the recent-facts exclusion already spaces them.
- **Mean, or a lower percentile.** The mean is dragged up by long pauses. A lower percentile only trades against the margin. The median is the least affected by odd answers.
- **A margin of 2 × pace, pace plus a fixed number of seconds, or the slowest share of recent answers.** At 2 × a fact worked out on fingers grades fast. A fixed number of seconds is wide for a quick learner and tight for a slow typist. A fixed share grades that share slow for ever, so levels could never settle at 4. With typical spread, 1.5 × grades about 1 in 5 of a mixed learner's right answers slow and about 1 in 20 of an evenly known pool.
- **No floor, or a minimum gap of pace plus 1 second.** Under a pace of 2 seconds the line falls below 3 seconds and ordinary variation breaks streaks for the learner doing best. The 3-second floor is not the old limit: it is never shown and never makes an answer slow, it only stops the relative line from dropping below what counts as knowing a fact cold.
- **A window of 20, 200, or a number of days.** At 20 the line follows the choice of tables in the last drill rather than the learner. At 200 a learner improving over two weeks is graded against who they were. A window in days empties over a holiday. 60 is three to four drills.
- **A stand-in limit before pace exists, or a pace from 5 answers.** A stand-in is a fixed limit at the moment it fits worst, while the learner is finding the keys, and would drop carried levels on the first day. A median of 5 is noisy. All-fast lasts about one drill, a miss still sets the level to 0, and a level gained in it is given back by the first slow outcome.
- **Softening or resetting carried levels.** The migration ships before the tables widen, so carried levels sit in the pool they were earned in and come round as often as before; a miss or a slow outcome corrects an inflated one. Softening would show learners and the Parent view progress lost overnight.
- **No cap, or discarding a long presentation.** The median tolerates a few long answer times, but the clock is unreliable across backgrounding in both directions, and a time that reads short could earn a fast outcome. Discarding would add a presentation that does not count to the drill and its record.
- **Pace fixed at the start of each drill.** It would delay the first pace to the third drill and needs a stored value; the line moves little within a drill at a window of 60.

## Consequences

- A learner who is equally slow on every fact gets almost no slow outcomes, and their levels reach 3 and 4 while their answers are still slow in absolute terms. This follows from judging a learner only against themselves: a high level means "right, and no slower than this learner usually is". Weighted repetition then spreads practice evenly, which suits that learner, and the falling pace is what shows their improvement in the Parent view. Making a level mean an absolute speed would need the fixed limit back.
- The rule needs one addition to the stored progress: the last 60 right answer times, leaving out those at the cap. What the Parent view trend needs is decided separately.
- 1.5 and 60 are the values to retune first if levels look wrong in practice.
